The War We Already Lost

September 6, 2026

Listen to this article
--:--

There was a war on intelligence, and we lost it. Not decisively, not all at once — but decisively enough that pretending otherwise is no longer interesting.

For most of history, intelligence was the trait we built our whole self-image around. Homo sapiens. The thinking animal. Every other species had claws, speed, camouflage. We had this. It was the thing that made the rest of the food chain irrelevant.

That is over now, or close enough. A model can outreason you, outwrite you, hold more of a problem in its head at once than you can, and do it before your coffee gets cold. This is not a controversial claim anymore. It is just Tuesday.

The instinct is to fight the framing — to find the one place intelligence still wins. Judgment. Taste. Creativity. Common sense. I’ve made versions of that argument myself. But most of those turn out to be intelligence with a nicer name, and the ground keeps moving out from under each one a little faster than the last.

So here’s the harder question. If intelligence was the thing, and the thing is gone — what’s left?

What Doesn’t Transfer

Start with what a model can do. It can describe grief precisely. It can write about loss with more craft than most people manage. Ask it what fear feels like and it will give you an answer better constructed than yours.

None of that means it has lost anything. It has processed a description of loss. It has not sat awake at 3am with the specific shape of an absence. There is no dread in it before a diagnosis, no relief after one. It can simulate the language of stakes without ever having any.

That is the gap. Not capability. Vulnerability — the capacity to be hurt, and to know that you are the one being hurt. A system that can be wounded has something at stake in its own continued existence. That is not a small thing.

It isn’t uniquely human in the broadest sense — animals suffer, grieve, fear, attach. What seems different in us is what happens afterward: the ability to carry an experience forward as part of a continuing self, return to it years later, reinterpret it, and let it change the person doing the remembering. Animals carry experience too. We seem unusually condemned to interpret it.

The Mistake Isn’t the Lesson

There’s a temptation to collapse this into something simpler: suffering is just learning from mistakes. You touch the stove, you don’t touch it again. Mistake made, lesson learned.

But that kind of learning is cold. It requires no self at all. A thermostat corrects. A chess engine registers a loss and feels precisely nothing while doing it. If suffering were just error-correction, AI would already have it in abundance — error-correction is the thing these systems do better than anyone alive.

What suffering adds is cost. Not “wrong output, adjust” but a self that has to live inside the wrongness for a while — the shame of having hurt someone, the years spent carrying a decision you can’t undo. That felt cost is what makes the lesson actually change you, rather than just becoming a fact you know. You can tell someone their mistake, and they can agree with you completely, and nothing moves — because agreement isn’t suffering. Real change starts when the mistake actually hurts to sit with.

But cost by itself isn’t enough either. Suffering without what comes next just curdles — plenty of people carry pain their entire lives and learn nothing from it except how to carry more of it. Kierkegaard said life is lived forwards but understood backwards. There’s no lesson available in the moment, only weight, and the weight has no shape yet. The shape comes later, once you’re far enough from it to hold it at a distance and ask what it meant.

So the loop needs more than mistake-and-correction:

Mistake → suffering (felt cost) → retrospection → progress.

Remove the middle term and you get a machine: mistake, correction, instantly, nothing paid, no self altered. Remove the last term and you get scar tissue: pain with nowhere to go. Either way, nobody was changed by it, because either nobody was in there to be changed, or nobody stayed long enough to find out what it meant.

Ray Dalio built an investment philosophy around a version of this loop: pain plus reflection equals progress. The insight isn’t really about markets. Pain without reflection is just damage; reflection without pain has nothing to work on. You need both, and a self durable enough to hold the distance between them.

Why the Machine Can’t Run the Loop

Today’s AI can process the concept of loss instantly and completely. What it does not appear to do is carry a loss for years and slowly become a different self because of it. There is no shortcut through lived time to earned understanding.

This is not a claim about processing power. It’s a claim about structure. Intelligence doesn’t require a self that persists and can be wounded. Suffering does.

And this isn’t a gap that better models close, on its own terms. Whatever suffering is, in us, it seems bound up with irreversibility — there is exactly one of you, you cannot reload yesterday, and a bad outcome costs you permanently, with no restore point. That irreversibility is what gives regret and mortality and even ordinary choice their weight. An AI instance can be paused, copied, rolled back, run in parallel a thousand times over. Whether something could still matter to a system built that way is an open question, not a solved one — but the human version of stakes is intensified by a fact that doesn’t obviously apply the same way to something reproducible and reversible by design.

That’s a strange thing to end on: the thing that makes you less than a machine may also be the thing that makes you irreplaceable. A model can be backed up. You can’t. Everything that follows from that is the tax on being the only copy. AI may end up beating us partly because it is reproducible, reversible, and scalable. We matter to ourselves partly because we aren’t.
